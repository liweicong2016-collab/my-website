Optical Transceivers, Act Two: From \u201cTotal Ban\u201d Panic to a \u201cProfit Shift Upstream\u201d Thesis on US Export Controls

From the FCC\u2019s July 26-50 order to September\u2019s soft-landing final rule, to the bipartisan September 25 bill turning to federal procurement \u2014 retracing three reversals in three months and decoding the Morgan Stanley note\u2019s policy logic.

The One-Line Verdict

  • Over the past three months, US controls on Chinese optical transceivers have run the full cycle of \u201cpanic \u2014 easing \u2014 escalation\u201d: the September 11 final rule landed softly, vindicating the \u201cbad news is priced in\u201d trade \u2014 but the bipartisan bill of September 25 moved the fight to federal procurement. This play still has an act two.
  • The real story isn\u2019t \u201cban or not ban\u201d \u2014 it\u2019s that next-gen optical profits are shifting from Chinese module makers upstream to US laser and chip suppliers.
  • Lasers hold certainty; module makers hold a policy option \u2014 and \u201centity-based vs. origin-based controls\u201d is the single most important variable in the whole story.

The final rule\u2019s (108-page) core logic: an FCC authorization can only be withheld when the equipment uses \u201clogical hardware\u201d made by a Covered List company. The FCC explicitly rejected the more aggressive proposal \u2014 it did not ban every company \u201ccontrolled by a foreign adversary.\u201d The rule takes effect October 13, applies only forward, and existing authorizations are untouched.

Key Signals

  • Controls spread from FCC equipment authorization to government procurement \u2014 the September 25 bill targets sensitive federal systems and names Innolight and Eoptolink.
  • ITI, the US tech industry association, wrote to the FCC on August 13 urging it to \u201cnot blacklist optical modules,\u201d arguing that a supply cutoff would slow data-center buildouts and hurt US AI leadership \u2014 Chinese vendors\u2019 market position is the hard constraint on how far the FCC can go.

2What the Morgan Stanley Note Actually Said

A cold splash first: this Morgan Stanley institutional-client briefing isn\u2019t public; everything circulating is second-hand retelling, and key figures \u2014 the 65% BOM exemption, the 2028/2029 timeline \u2014 can\u2019t be verified. Read what follows as \u201cunverified claims,\u201d not facts to bet on.

The retelling\u2019s main points:

  • 3.2T faces restrictions, while China-made 800G and 1.6T are unaffected \u2014 core products stay safe.
  • 65% US BOM content earns an exemption: a Chinese module can be imported if 65% of its BOM value comes from the US; modules using US DSPs plus US lasers are already near that threshold. (This lines up with the location-based list logic Citi described: \u226565% US domestic content, rising to 75% from 2029.)
  • InP (indium phosphide) substrates are the variable to watch \u2014 not the modules themselves.
  • Timeline: 3.2T expected to start ramping in 2028, broader adoption around 2029, then volume.
  • The overall shift: from \u201creplace Chinese supply immediately\u201d to \u201cdeciding where next-gen products get built.\u201d

Near-term take: US-listed names get the better sentiment; A-share Innolight and Eoptolink get their \u201cbad news is priced in\u201d moment \u2014 the feared total ban never arrived, 800G/1.6T core products are safe, and orders for the next two years are unaffected.

Long-term take: profits migrate upstream. InP EML/CW laser sources are the biggest winners \u2014 good for LITE, COHR, and AAOI with its US capacity; DSP and TIA/driver players AVGO and MRVL are more entrenched; Chinese module makers become \u201cUS chips plus Chinese assembly\u201d; InP substrates and indium become the chain\u2019s most fragile link \u2014 and Beijing\u2019s most direct retaliation lever.

3What Public Data Confirms

  • 800G/1.6T are ramping: TrendForce expects ~63 million 800G+ units in 2026, 2.6\u00d7 the 2025 figure; the AI optical module market is about $26 billion, up 57% YoY. The \u201ccore products are safe\u201d call lines up with fundamentals.
  • The bottleneck really is upstream: EML/CW lasers are tight, Nvidia has pre-locked large EML capacity with lead times into 2027 and beyond; InP substrates face a 70%+ shortage, orders queued into 2028, prices up ~2.5\u00d7 in two years.
  • Seven of the top ten: seven of the world\u2019s top ten optical module vendors are Chinese; Chinese vendors hold over 70% of 800G/1.6T high-speed shipments; Innolight alone holds ~27% of global data-center modules. Coherent and Lumentum have the tech but not the capacity to fill that gap.

My Read on the Retelling

  • The \u201cbad news is priced in\u201d thesis holds only if controls stay limited to 3.2T and 800G/1.6T keep their exemptions \u2014 tighten the exemption criteria and this logic breaks first.
  • Profits are indeed shifting upstream, but pricing power isn\u2019t one-way: CSPs are all signing long-term capacity contracts; upstream suppliers can\u2019t raise prices without answering to their biggest customers.
  • InP substrates are a retaliation lever, but it\u2019s double-edged: China itself is short on substrates (demand of 2.6\u20133 million wafers vs. ~750k of effective capacity) \u2014 hitting back would hurt its own module makers first.
  • 3.2T ramping in 2028 is technically reasonable, but CPO/LPO could bypass part of pluggable demand \u2014 Nvidia\u2019s CPO switches are already in volume production, a more structural variable than any FCC rule.

AAOI (US-made) and Fabrinet (Thailand) absorbing the share shift holds up logically \u2014 with one trap: under location-based rules, output from Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia counts as \u201cforeign produced\u201d just like output from China. If controls go by origin, the Southeast Asia re-routing play is dead too.

InP substrates and indium: the chain\u2019s most fragile link, capacity concentrated in China, Beijing\u2019s most direct retaliation lever \u2014 but retaliation cuts both ways. Domestic 6-inch lines like Yunnan Germanium\u2019s are patching exactly this gap.

5The Key Variable: Entity-Based or Origin-Based

One Ruler for Every Policy

  • Entity-based: Innolight and Eoptolink take the hit; AAOI, Coherent, Lumentum, and Southeast Asian capacity absorb the share shift.
  • Origin-based (the location-based list logic): everything built \u201cabroad\u201d counts \u2014 Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia capacity is restricted the same way, only US-made product is truly safe. The share-shift thesis collapses.

Every future policy text deserves to be measured against this one ruler.

6Closing

September 11\u2019s soft landing priced in the \u201cbad news is out,\u201d but the September 25 bill says this play has an act two. Near term it\u2019s about sentiment and orders \u2014 800G/1.6T really are safe for the next two years; long term it\u2019s about who controls upstream \u2014 lasers and DSPs hold certainty, module makers hold a policy option.

And the line worth remembering isn\u2019t \u201cban or not ban\u201d: the substance of this contest is next-gen optical profits migrating to US upstream lasers and chips. Understanding that matters more than guessing the tightness of any single rule.
