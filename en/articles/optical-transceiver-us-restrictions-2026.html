Optical Transceivers' Second Half: From "Blanket Ban" Panic to the Upstream Profit Shift

From the July FCC 26-50 order to the mild September final rule, then to the September 25 bipartisan bill turning to federal procurement — recapping three U-turns in three months and unpacking the policy logic of the Morgan Stanley note.

2026-10-02 ~9 min read Optical Transceivers · Export Controls · Supply Chain
Optical Transceivers FCC Innolight Eoptolink Supply Chain

Key Takeaways

  • Over the past three months, US restrictions on Chinese optical transceivers have run the full "panic–reprieve–escalation" cycle: the September 11 final rule landed softly, delivering the "bad news priced in" moment — but the September 25 bipartisan bill moved the battlefield to federal government procurement — this play has a second act.
  • The real story isn't "banned or not" — it's that the profits of next-generation optical communications are migrating from Chinese module makers to upstream US laser and chip suppliers.
  • Lasers hold certainty; module makers hold a policy option; and whether regulation is entity-based or origin-based is the single most important variable in the whole story.

The final rule (108-page full text) boils down to this: only equipment using "logical hardware" produced by Covered List companies could be denied FCC authorization. The FCC explicitly rejected the more aggressive proposal — it did not blacklist all companies "controlled by foreign adversaries." The rule takes effect October 13, applies prospectively only, and leaves existing authorizations untouched.

Key Signals

  • The restrictions have spread from FCC equipment authorization to government procurement — the September 25 bill targets federal sensitive systems and names Innolight and Eoptolink.
  • US tech industry association ITI wrote to the FCC on August 13 asking it not to blacklist optical transceivers, arguing that supply cuts would slow data-center deployment and hurt US AI leadership — Chinese vendors' market position is the hard constraint keeping the FCC from coming down harder.

2What the Morgan Stanley Note Says

First, a reality check: this Morgan Stanley institutional-client briefing isn't public — everything circulating is secondhand retelling. Key figures like the 65% BOM exemption and the 2028/2029 timeline can't be verified — read what follows as "unverified claims," not facts to bet on.

The gist of the retelling:

  • Restrictions kick in at 3.2T; China-made 800G and 1.6T unaffected — flagship products are safe.
  • 65% US BOM content qualifies for exemption: Chinese modules whose BOM value is 65% US-sourced can be imported; Chinese modules using US DSPs plus US lasers are already close to this threshold. (Consistent with the location-based list logic in Citi's note: ≥65% US domestic content required, rising to 75% from 2029.)
  • InP (indium phosphide) substrates are the real variable to watch, not the modules themselves.
  • Timeline: 3.2T expected to ramp in 2028, with broader adoption around 2029, then volume.
  • The overall thrust shifts from "immediately replacing Chinese supply" to "deciding where next-generation products get made".

Short-term scenario: better sentiment for US stocks; for A-shares, Innolight and Eoptolink get their "bad news priced in" moment — the feared blanket ban never materialized, 800G/1.6T flagship products are safe, and orders over the next two years are unaffected.

Long-term scenario: profits migrate upstream. InP EML/CW light sources are the biggest beneficiaries — positive for $LITE, $COHR, and AAOI with US capacity; AVGO and MRVL's positions in DSP, TIA/drivers get even stronger; Chinese module makers become "US chips plus Chinese assembly"; InP substrates and indium become the chain's weakest link — and China's most direct countermeasure lever.

3What Public Data Corroborates

  • 800G/1.6T are ramping: TrendForce expects ~63 million 800G-and-above units shipped in 2026, 2.6× 2025; the AI optical-transceiver market is ~$26 billion, up 57% YoY. The "flagship products are safe" call lines up with the fundamentals.
  • The bottleneck is indeed upstream: EML/CW lasers are in short supply, Nvidia has already locked up large chunks of EML capacity, lead times stretch past 2027; the InP substrate shortfall exceeds 70%, order books run to 2028, prices up ~2.5× in two years.
  • Seven of the top ten: seven of the world's top-ten optical-transceiver vendors are Chinese; Chinese vendors account for over 70% of high-speed 800G/1.6T shipments; Innolight alone holds ~27% of global data-center transceivers. Coherent and Lumentum's technology is solid, but their capacity can't fill the gap.

My Take on the Retelling

  • "Bad news priced in" holds only if restrictions stay at 3.2T and 800G/1.6T keep their exemption — if the exemption tightens, this thesis breaks first.
  • Profits moving upstream is the right direction, but pricing power isn't one-directional: CSPs are all signing long-term contracts to lock up capacity, so upstream players need their big customers' blessing to raise prices too.
  • InP substrates are a countermeasure chip, but it's double-edged: China is short on substrates itself (demand of 2.6–3 million wafers vs. only ~750K of effective capacity) — playing it would hurt its own module makers first.
  • 3.2T ramping in 2028 is technically reasonable, but CPO/LPO could bypass some pluggable demand — Nvidia's CPO switches are already in production, a more structural variable than FCC rules.

AAOI (US manufacturing) and Fabrinet (Thailand) absorbing the share shift — the logic holds, but there's a trap: under location-based standards, production in Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia counts as "foreign produced" just like China. If enforcement is truly origin-based, the Southeast Asia re-routing path is a dead end too.

InP substrates and indium: the chain's weakest link, with capacity concentrated in China — China's most direct countermeasure lever; but retaliation cuts both ways. Domestic 6-inch lines like Yunnan Germanium's are plugging exactly this gap.

5The Key Variable: Entity-Based or Origin-Based

One Yardstick for All Policies

  • Entity-based: Innolight and Eoptolink under pressure; AAOI, Coherent, Lumentum, and Southeast Asia capacity capture the share shift.
  • Origin-based (location-based list logic): anything produced "abroad" counts — Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia capacity gets restricted too; only US domestic manufacturing is truly safe — and the share-shift logic largely breaks down.

Every future policy text deserves to be measured with this yardstick.

6Conclusion

The mild September 11 landing delivered the "bad news priced in" moment, but the September 25 proposal shows this play has a second act. Short term: watch sentiment and orders — 800G/1.6T are genuinely safe for the next two years; long term: watch who controls the upstream — lasers and DSPs hold certainty, module makers hold a policy option.

And one line worth remembering beyond "banned or not": the essence of this game is the migration of next-generation optical-communications profits to upstream US laser and chip suppliers. Seeing that matters far more than guessing the tightness of each individual policy.
